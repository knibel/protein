## 1. Which pairings are documented, with numbers

### Takeaway
Pairings of lysine-poor foods (cereals, nuts, seeds) with lysine-rich but sulfur-amino-acid-poor foods (legumes) are well supported in principle. The best-quantified example I could verify is pea + rice, with a modelled optimum of about 59 % pea and 41 % rice by protein and a DIAAS of about 84 (young-child pattern). Hard published numbers for the other classic pairs (rice+beans, hummus, peanut+wheat, corn+beans, soy+rice) are mostly missing from the sources I could open. The app should therefore compute them from its own amino acid data and not quote them as published values.

### Cited Findings
- Pea/rice model (Herreman et al. 2020, Food Sci Nutr 8:5379): the blend reaches a maximum DIAAS of 84 at 41 % rice protein and 59 % pea protein, using the 0.5–3 y pattern. This is a calculation from mean literature data. It is not a feeding trial. — [PMC7590266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590266/)
- Same paper, single-protein DIAAS (0.5–3 y pattern): pea 70 (limiting: Met+Cys), rice 47 (limiting: Lys). DIAA ratios in the paper: pea Lys 110, Met+Cys 70, Trp 77, Val 83, Leu 87. Rice Lys 47, Thr 75, Leu 80, Met+Cys 104, Trp 114. The linear mixing model is DIAA_blend = sum of (protein share × DIAA ratio), so the optimum sits where the two limiting ratios cross. — [PMC7590266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590266/)
- Gorissen et al. 2018 (Amino Acids 50:1685), isolates, g per 100 g raw material (not per g protein). Lys / Met / Cys / Thr: pea 4.7 / 0.3 / 0.2 / 2.5; brown rice 1.9 / 2.0 / 0.6 / 2.3; soy 3.4 / 0.3 / 0.2 / 2.3; wheat 1.1 / 0.7 / 0.7 / 1.8; corn 1.0 / 1.1 / 0.3 / 1.8; potato 4.8 / 1.3 / 0.3 / 4.1. Tryptophan was not measured because acid hydrolysis destroys it. — [PMC6245118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6245118/)
- Gorissen 2018 conclusion: 50/50 blends of lysine-poor corn, brown rice or hemp with methionine-poor soy, microalgae or pea give intermediate Lys and Met. They need about 10–90 % more protein to meet essential amino acid needs, versus 2–4 times more for single sources. Potato was the only isolate meeting the WHO/FAO/UNU pattern for all essential amino acids. — [PMC6245118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6245118/)
- Gorissen 2018 abstract: Lys and Met are typically lower in plant protein (about 3.6 % and 1.0 % on average) than in animal protein. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00726-018-2640-5), via search summary.
- Pig study of cooked pulse/cereal blends (Han et al. 2021, Plants): DIAAS (older child/adult pattern) was 93 for mung bean, 78 for adzuki bean, 22 for millet and 16 for adlay. Blends gave 66 for mung bean + millet and 51 for adzuki + adlay. Complementation was real but incomplete. Lysine true ileal digestibility ranged from 90 % (adzuki) to 70 % (millet). — [Massey repository](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/items/161a5c88-26e0-4f8a-a088-573d0bfef24d), [PMC8541063](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8541063/). I got these from a search summary only, because the PMC full text was blocked.
- PDCAAS of single foods (Hoffman & Falvo 2004, J Sports Sci Med 3:118): egg, milk, casein, whey and soy protein 1.00; beef 0.92; black beans 0.75; peanuts 0.52; wheat gluten 0.25. The paper gives no PDCAAS for any food combination and does not mention rice + beans. Its conclusion: "With a proper combination of sources, vegetable proteins may provide similar benefits as protein from animal sources." The fetch tool flagged uncertain table alignment, so verify before quoting individual values. — [PMC3905294](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3905294/)
- Pea and rice concentrate, single-protein values from a Nutrition & Metabolism 2016 table (doi 10.1186/s12986-016-0124-8; authors not confirmed, I did not read the table itself): pea PDCAAS 0.89 / DIAAS 0.82 (Met+Cys limiting); rice protein concentrate PDCAAS 0.42 / DIAAS 0.37 (Lys limiting). Note that the DIAAS of 0.70 for pea in Herreman differs from 0.82 in this table. DIAAS for the same food varies by source, age pattern and processing. — [Nutr Metab table](https://nutritionandmetabolism.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12986-016-0124-8/tables/1) (search summary only)
- Biologische Wertigkeit (BW) of food mixtures, German tradition: 1/2 beans + 1/2 corn gives BW 99. Single values are potato 76, corn 72, beans 72. 1/3 egg + 2/3 potato gives 136. The source is a 2007 document table of unclear origin, so I rate it low quality. Individual BW values differ widely between sources (for example soy 74, pea 65, wheat 54 elsewhere). BW is a nitrogen-retention measure in rats and is not directly an amino acid score. — [derstandard.at PDF](https://images.derstandard.at/20071115/Protein.pdf), [Quarks](https://www.quarks.de/?p=18763), [gesundheits-lexikon](https://www.gesundheits-lexikon.com/Sport/Proteine-fuer-Muskelaufbau-und-Regeneration-im-Sport/Tierisches-vs-pflanzliches-Eiweiss-was-Sportler-wissen-sollten) (all via search summary)
- Ernährungs Umschau 2017 (Körnerleguminosen): peas and beans, and somewhat less clearly lupins, complement the lysine-poorer cereal protein well. Lupin protein is rich in arginine. — [EU 09/2017 M500–M505](https://www.ernaehrungs-umschau.de/fileadmin/Ernaehrungs-Umschau/pdfs/Freie_PDF/EU09_2017_M500-M505.pdf) (search snippet; the PDF itself was unreadable to the tool)
- Counterpoint on how special grain+legume pairing really is: the vProtein study (2011) found no statistically significant bias toward grain/legume pairs among optimal amino acid complements. This implies many other combinations also work, for example legume + nut/seed, or potato + legume. — [PMC3081312](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3081312/) (search summary only)
- Hummus / sesame + chickpea: only a retailer blog supports the qualitative claim (chickpea rich in Lys, low in Met; sesame the reverse). I found no measured score for hummus. — [Country Life Foods blog](https://countrylifefoods.com/blogs/beans-and-legumes/hummus-nutrition-chickpeas-tahini-a-pantry-guide) (marketing source, low authority)
- Commercial claim only: Ingredion markets a pea+rice isolate blend as PDCAAS 1.0 and states it did not measure this in vivo. — [Ingredion](https://ingredion.com/ingredients/na/pea--rice-isolate-1780-70001152.html)

### Inferences
- Because a mixture's amino acid profile is the protein-weighted average of its components, the app can compute any pair itself. Per essential amino acid (EAA) i: `mix_i = w*a_i + (1-w)*b_i`, where w is the protein fraction of food A and a_i, b_i are mg EAA per g protein. Score = min over EAA of mix_i / reference_i. The optimum w is where the two limiting lines cross, as Herreman did for pea and rice. Pea+rice is a good unit test: pea 0.59 + rice 0.41 should return about 0.84 under the 0.5–3 y pattern and the digestibility data used by Herreman. The expected result changes if you use other data.
- Pairs that the algorithm should rank highly, based on the limiting amino acids below: cereal + legume; legume + sesame/other seeds; corn or rice + pea or soy; wheat + lupin or pea; pseudo-cereal (quinoa, amaranth) + legume is less necessary. Peanut + wheat is plausible (both Lys-limited), but that is a poor pair because both are lysine-limited. The app should not present it as a classic complement unless the data support it. Peanut is a legume botanically but behaves like a nut in amino acid terms (Lys-limited; the PDCAAS of 0.52 above is consistent with that).
- Corn (maize) is limited by lysine and, in the traditional diet, also by tryptophan. Corn + beans is therefore good for Lys, and Trp is usually adequate in beans [UNVERIFIED for exact numbers].
- Rice + beans: rice is Lys-limited and beans are Met+Cys-limited, so the pair is plausible. I found no published combined score, so compute it.

### Gaps
- No published combined PDCAAS or DIAAS for rice+beans, hummus, peanut+wheat, corn+beans or soy+rice could be retrieved. Young & Pellett 1994 (Am J Clin Nutr 59:1203S) is the classic source. I only saw its abstract, which says plant protein blends can meet human amino acid needs. Its full text was not available.
- Ben-Harb 2018 (wheat/legume blends) and Rutherfurd 2015 (DIAAS of foods) were not found. Treat any numbers attributed to them as unverified.
- Hoffman & Falvo gave no combination data, despite the brief. Do not cite them for pairings.

## 2. Limiting amino acids in major plant foods

### Takeaway
Cereals are limited by lysine (and secondarily threonine), legumes by sulfur amino acids (methionine + cysteine, sometimes also tryptophan and valine). Nuts and seeds are generally lysine-limited, and sesame is Lys-poor/Met-rich. The first limiting amino acid depends on which reference pattern (age group) is used, so the app should state the pattern.

### Cited Findings
- Mariotti & Gardner 2019 (Nutrients 11:2661): lysine is "present in much lower than optimal proportions for human needs in grains"; sulfur amino acids are "proportionally very slightly lower in legumes than would be optimal". Tryptophan is similar in plant and animal proteins and sufficient relative to requirements. Plant leucine averages about 8 g/100 g protein (range 6–14), versus about 9.5 for animal protein. The authors conclude that concern about amino acid deficiency is largely overstated and that legumes, nuts and seeds suffice for protein adequacy in adults. — [PMC6893534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6893534/)
- Herreman table: pea limited by Met+Cys, rice by Lys, with Trp being second-lowest in pea (77) and Thr second-lowest in rice (75). — [PMC7590266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590266/)
- Gorissen 2018: wheat and corn are Lys-poor; pea and soy are Met-poor; oat, lupin and wheat are low in both Lys and Met. — [PMC6245118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6245118/)
- Swiss "Ernährungsmedizin" conference report 2024: cereals typically lack lysine, legumes methionine and cysteine; mice fed cereal + legumes grew like casein-fed mice. This is a mouse study and is a secondary report. — [rosenfluh.ch](https://rosenfluh.ch/ernaehrungsmedizin-2024-01/pflanzliche-ernaehrung-im-trend-pflanzenbasierte-ernaehrung-was-ist-zu-beachten_)
- Soy is the one plant food usually called "complete" in German consumer advice. — [Pharmazeutische Zeitung](https://www.pharmazeutische-zeitung.de/eiweissbedarf-pflanzlich-decken-darauf-kommt-es-an-162336/)
- FAO 2013 (FAO Food and Nutrition Paper 92) reference patterns, mg per g protein, children >3 y, adolescents and adults: His 16, Ile 30, Leu 61, Lys 48, SAA (Met+Cys) 23, AAA (Phe+Tyr) 41, Thr 25, Trp 6.6, Val 40. For 0.5–3 y: His 20, Ile 32, Leu 66, Lys 57, SAA 27, AAA 52, Thr 31, Trp 8.5, Val 43. [UNVERIFIED in this session: the FAO PDF returned HTTP 403, so this comes from my background knowledge. Check against [FAO](https://www.fao.org/3/i3124e/i3124e.pdf) before hard-coding.]

### Inferences
- A per-amino-acid table in the app should use SAA (Met+Cys) and AAA (Phe+Tyr) as combined pools, as FAO does. Counting Cys and Tyr alone would break the comparison.
- The app can show the "limiting amino acid" of the picked food and name the partner that supplies it. This is an explanation that users understand, and it makes the algorithm checkable.

### Gaps
- No authoritative single table for all foods the app contains was retrieved. Use the app's own source (for example USDA / BLS / FAO INFOODS) and document it.

## 3. Digestibility, antinutritional factors, and why the app must state its basis

### Takeaway
Raw legumes and unheated plant proteins are digested less well than cooked ones, and different studies disagree by several percentage points for the same food. A mixture score computed from total amino acid content overstates quality for poorly digested foods. The app should state the reference pattern, whether values are per g protein, and whether the food is raw (dry) or cooked.

### Cited Findings
- DIAAS (FAO 2013) replaces PDCAAS: it uses ileal (not faecal) digestibility of each amino acid and does not truncate at 1.0. PDCAAS caps at 1.0, so soy isolate and whey isolate both score 1.0. — [Wikipedia PDCAAS](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/PDCAAS) (secondary)
- Herreman 2020: for cooked or processed foods, reactive (available) lysine rather than total lysine should be used for DIAAS, and scores vary a lot within the same protein source. — [PMC7590266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590266/), via search summary
- Pea: in vitro protein digestibility rose from about 80–83 % raw to about 86–87 % cooked. The resistant proteins include PA2 albumin, trypsin inhibitor, lectin and lipoxygenase. — [Fundación Bengoa](https://www.fundacionbengoa.org/novedades/publicaciones/articulos-libros-monografias-colaboraciones/protein-digestibility-and-trypsin-inhibitor-activity-of-legume-seeds/)
- Common bean (Cornell thesis): protein digestibility 79.1 % raw, 85.0 % cooked at 87 °C, 89.7 % at 100 °C; trypsin inhibitor activity was negatively correlated with digestibility (r² = 0.86). Thesis-level source. — [Cornell eCommons](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstream/handle/1813/29093/Pazlopez,%20Claudia%20M.%20-%20%20Research%20Honors%20Thesis.pdf?sequence=2)
- Jaffé 1950 (rats): red kidney beans about 56 % true digestibility raw vs 80 % autoclaved; the trypsin inhibitor did not explain all the improvement. Old study, via the same search summary.
- Mariotti & Gardner 2019: ileal digestibility is 89–92 % for soy isolate, pea protein, wheat flour and lupin flour, against 90–94 % for meat, 91 % for egg and 95 % for milk. The authors say the plant–animal difference is only a few percent, and that raw or lightly heated plant samples represent the worst case. — [PMC6893534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6893534/)
- Han 2021: lysine digestibility was lower in cooked millet (70 %) than adzuki bean (90 %), so cereal Lys is not only low but also less available. — [Massey](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/items/161a5c88-26e0-4f8a-a088-573d0bfef24d)
- Extrusion raised in vitro digestibility in a wheat-legume snack study (28–35 % raw flour mixes vs 60–65 % extrudates; small conference abstract, low weight). — [Nutrition Society NZ abstract](https://nutr2015.p.events4you.currinda.com/days/2015-12-02/abstract/336)
- Phytate: none of the retrieved sources quantified its effect on amino acid availability. Phytate matters mainly for minerals (iron, zinc), so I would not put it in the amino acid logic except as a general note [UNVERIFIED].

### Inferences
- Rationale for the UI note: protein content and amino acid composition differ strongly between dry and cooked food because cooking adds water (dry legumes about 20–25 g protein per 100 g, cooked about 7–9 g per 100 g [UNVERIFIED approximate figures from general knowledge]). Per g protein, the amino acid pattern changes little with water content, so per-g-protein scores are comparable across states. Quantities (g per portion) are not.
- Score in the app should therefore be: (a) based on mg amino acid per g protein, (b) labelled with the reference pattern, and (c) clearly say "gegart" or "roh/trocken" for each food. If digestibility factors are available, apply one factor per food; otherwise state that digestibility is not included, so the scores are "Aminosäurescore (chemisch)" rather than DIAAS or PDCAAS. Do not use the terms PDCAAS or DIAAS unless the digestibility correction is actually applied.

### Gaps
- No source with side-by-side raw vs cooked DIAAS for the same food. Trypsin inhibitor and heat data come only from digestibility studies.
- Phytate effect on amino acids not found.

## 4. Treatment of foods with trivial protein content; realistic mixing ratio

### Takeaway
A ranking should filter partners by protein content (and ideally by protein per portion or per energy), because a food with trivially little protein can show a perfect score per g protein but contribute nothing. No published threshold exists; the cutoff below is a design choice. The realistic mix is set by the foods' protein shares in an actual meal, roughly 40–60 % of the protein from each partner for the model pairs found.

### Cited Findings
- Quarks makes the practical point: egg + potato reaches BW 136, but potatoes contain only about 2 % protein, so the combination is impractical for athletes. — [Quarks](https://www.quarks.de/?p=18763) (via search summary)
- Pea/rice optimum is about 59:41 by protein; Gorissen used 50/50 by protein as an illustration. — [PMC7590266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590266/), [PMC6245118](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6245118/)
- Mariotti & Gardner's substitution model uses 40 % of protein from legumes, nuts and seeds, with the rest from foods already eaten. — [PMC6893534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6893534/)
- DGE (via Pharmazeutische Zeitung) gives portion examples: tofu 100 g about 16 g protein, cooked wholegrain pasta 200 g about 12 g, one slice of wholegrain bread (50 g) 3.5 g. — [Pharmazeutische Zeitung](https://www.pharmazeutische-zeitung.de/eiweissbedarf-pflanzlich-decken-darauf-kommt-es-an-162336/)
- No source gave a legume:cereal ratio. The Pharmazeutische Zeitung article names no ratio. [Searched; not found]

### Inferences (design suggestions, not published rules)
- Exclude oils, pure sugars, most vegetables and fruit, and drinks as partner candidates. Suggested filter: at least about 5 g protein per 100 g (as eaten), or at least about 10 % of energy from protein. The numbers are my suggestion and should be tuned by looking at the output list. Two alternatives: set the cutoff on protein per typical portion, or require that the partner supply at least about 20–30 % of the mix protein at a realistic portion size.
- Alternatively, do not drop low-protein foods but compute the realistic share: if the optimal w demands more partner protein than a portion can plausibly provide, show the pair as "theoretisch" or hide it.
- Report the optimal ratio as protein share (for example "etwa 60 % der Eiweißmenge aus A, 40 % aus B") and convert to grams of food using each food's protein per 100 g. A ratio in grams of food would mislead because protein density differs by a factor of 10 between dry and cooked forms.
- Because the mix score is flat near the optimum (it is a min of two lines), offer a range, for example the weights where score >= 90 % of the maximum, rather than a single ratio.
- Suggested labels (my proposal): score >= 100 % of the reference pattern in all EAA = "deckt alle essenziellen Aminosäuren"; improvement of the limiting-AA ratio by a defined amount over the picked food alone = "ergänzt gut". Use the gain in the limiting ratio, not just the final score, so a pair is not credited for what the first food already had.
- Sanity checks for the algorithm: pea + rice optimum about 59:41 giving about 0.84; a food paired with itself must have gain 0; the best partner for a Lys-limited cereal should be a Lys-rich legume; the best partner for a legume should be Met+Cys-rich (cereal, sesame, Brazil nut, etc.).

### Gaps
- No published minimum-protein threshold for complementarity features, and no published mixing-ratio rule for general foods.

## 5. Health-communication disclaimers and German terminology

### Takeaway
Present the feature as an educational aid, not as dietary advice. Official bodies say a varied plant diet covers amino acid needs and that complementary proteins need not be eaten at the same meal. The DGE still lists protein/indispensable amino acids among potentially critical nutrients for vegan diets and advises counselling for vulnerable groups.

### Cited Findings
- DGE position on vegan diet (updated 2024): for healthy adults a well-planned vegan diet can be health-promoting provided vitamin B12 is supplemented. Protein / essential amino acids remain on the list of potentially critical nutrients. For children, adolescents, pregnant and breastfeeding women and older people the DGE gives neither a clear recommendation nor a warning against, and strongly advises qualified counselling. Certainty of evidence is rated mostly low to very low. — [DGE Position 2024 (Langversion, PIK mirror)](https://publications.pik-potsdam.de/pubman/item/item_29966_1/component/file_29967/DGE_Position_Vegan_2024_Langversion.pdf), [Ernährungs Umschau EU07/2024](https://ernaehrungs-umschau.de/fileadmin/Ernaehrungs-Umschau/pdfs/pdf_2024/07_24/EU07_2024_M382_M386.pdf), [DGE FAQ 2023](https://dge.de/fileadmin/dok/gesunde-ernaehrung/faq/DGE-FAQ-Vegane_Ernährung-2023.pdf), critique at [Lebensmittelpraxis](https://lebensmittelpraxis.de/industrie-aktuell/40441-dge-positionspapier-proveg-unterschlaegt-schwachpunkte-veganer-ernaehrung.html). All via search summaries; I did not read the DGE documents themselves, so verify the wording.
- Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics 2016 (Melina, Craig, Levin; J Acad Nutr Diet 116:1970): appropriately planned vegetarian, including vegan, diets are healthful, nutritionally adequate and may provide health benefits, and are suitable for all life stages. — [Andrews Univ. record](https://digitalcommons.andrews.edu/pubs/648), DOI 10.1016/j.jand.2016.09.025. The claim that plant proteins eaten across the day supply enough amino acids, and that same-meal pairing is not needed, comes from blog-style secondary summaries [UNVERIFIED against the paper text]. Be aware that the 2009 ADA paper is the one a Tufts newsletter cites for timing. The 2015 PubMed entry (PMID 25911342) showed a retraction-type tag in one search summary. I could not resolve this. Check before citing the 2015 version; cite the 2016 paper.
- Verbraucherzentrale Bayern (via Pharmazeutische Zeitung): plant foods usually lack single essential amino acids, soy is the exception, so combine protein sources; grains and legumes complement each other; the combination need not be in the same meal ("Es reicht, sie über den Tag zu verteilen"). Examples: Linsenbolognese mit Vollkornpasta, Erbsensuppe mit Brot. DGE protein reference: 0.8 g/kg body weight for ages 19–64, 1.0 g/kg from 65. — [Pharmazeutische Zeitung](https://www.pharmazeutische-zeitung.de/eiweissbedarf-pflanzlich-decken-darauf-kommt-es-an-162336/)
- Mariotti & Gardner: "a reasonable variety of sources over the course of the day appears to be appropriate"; same-meal mixing is "a practical way to secure long-term adequacy" if total protein intake is low. EPIC-Oxford lysine intake of about 43 mg/kg in vegans vs an estimated average requirement of 30 mg/kg. — [PMC6893534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6893534/)
- A contrasting finding: a 2019 DIAAS comparison in vegetarian and non-vegetarian athletes reports lower protein quality in vegetarian diets. — [PubMed 31835510](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31835510/) (search summary only)
- A clinical trial registration states that the 24-hour-window claim "has never been tested". — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07151625](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07151625) (registry description, not data)

### German terminology (correct forms; the DGE/Standard usage is "essenzielle Aminosäuren", and "essentielle" is also correct and common)
- Amino acids: Lysin, Methionin, Cystein (also Cystin as the dimer; in German texts "Cystein" and "Cystin" both appear, and the newer spelling "Cystein" fits), Threonin, Tryptophan, Isoleucin, Leucin, Valin, Phenylalanin, Tyrosin, Histidin.
- Pools: Methionin + Cystein = "schwefelhaltige Aminosäuren" ; Phenylalanin + Tyrosin = "aromatische Aminosäuren"; Leucin, Isoleucin, Valin = "verzweigtkettige Aminosäuren (BCAA)".
- Classification: eight indispensable amino acids (Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr, Trp, Val); histidine is counted as the ninth (indispensable) in modern FAO/WHO usage; Cystein and Tyrosin are "bedingt essenziell" (spared by Met and Phe respectively). Sources disagree on whether histidine is listed. — [Dr. Gumpert list](https://www.dr-gumpert.de/html/aminosaeuren_liste.html), [chemie.de](https://www.chemie.de/lexikon/Essentielle_Aminos%C3%A4ure.html), [Wikibooks Biochemie](https://de.wikibooks.org/wiki/Biochemie_und_Pathobiochemie:_Aminos%C3%A4uren-Stoffwechsel), all via search summary. I did not find a DGE page defining the list.
- Concepts: "limitierende Aminosäure" (first limiting amino acid); "Aminosäurenmuster" or "Aminosäurenprofil" (the Pharmazeutische Zeitung spells it "Aminosäurenbedarf"; the form "Aminosäuremuster" is also in use, so pick one consistently); "biologische Wertigkeit" (BW; reference whole egg or the egg/milk mixture, with BW above 100 possible in mixtures); "Proteinqualität" or "Eiweißqualität"; "Proteinverdaulichkeit"; "Eiweißergänzung" or "Ergänzungswert" or "Ergänzungswirkung" for complementarity (Ernährungs Umschau uses "Ergänzungswirkung"); "Protein-Score"/"Aminosäure-Score" for the chemical score; "Eiweiß" and "Protein" are both used by DGE and consumer advice.
- Suggested wording for the app: "Aminosäurewert (chemisch, pro g Eiweiß, bezogen auf das FAO-Referenzmuster 2013)". Avoid "vollständiges/unvollständiges Protein" as a hard label; German advice itself says "pflanzliche Lebensmittel enthalten meist alle, aber teils in geringerer Menge".

### Inferences
- Suggested disclaimer content: (1) values are calculated from food-composition data and are not individual advice; (2) a varied diet over the day is sufficient, so pairs need not be eaten together; (3) the ranking is about amino acid pattern, not about total protein amount, energy, micronutrients or allergens (nuts, peanut, sesame, soy and lupin are allergens); (4) more caution and professional counselling for children, pregnant or breastfeeding women, older people, and people with kidney disease or other medical conditions; (5) vitamin B12 for vegan diets.
- Do not claim that a pair is "complete" or "perfect" or that it "replaces animal protein" as a health claim.

### Gaps
- I could not read the DGE 2024 position text, the Ernährungs Umschau article text, or the AND paper text; quotes above come from search summaries and should be re-checked.
- No Verbraucherzentrale primary page on amino acid pairing was found; the Verbraucherzentrale statement is via the Pharmazeutische Zeitung.
- No German-language official glossary for "Eiweißergänzung" found; the term is common in the Ernährungs Umschau and older textbooks but I have no direct citation for a formal definition.
